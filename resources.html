🖥6.2 Interactive Classroom Tools
📊Mentimeter

Purpose: Pre-class quiz, real-time polls, post-test, exit tickets.

How to use in this lesson ▼
  1. Go to mentimeter.com and sign in.
  2. Click New Presentation → choose Quiz or Multiple Choice slide type.
  3. Enter each of the 5 pre-test questions from Section 2.
  4. Share the join code (e.g., menti.com + code) on the projected screen.
  5. Students enter the code on their phones and submit answers.
  6. Results appear live — use the bar chart to reveal correct answers and trigger pair discussion.
  7. Repeat the same presentation for the 3-question post-test in Section 3c.
  8. Add a final Open Ended slide for Exit Ticket Q1 (one-word summary).
📌Padlet

Purpose: Group poster gallery wall; peer feedback posts; take-away reflection wall.

How to use in this lesson ▼
  1. Go to padlet.com and create a free account.
  2. Click Make a Padlet → choose Wall layout.
  3. Title it: "PSY101 Theory Synthesis Gallery Wall".
  4. Share the link or QR code with students before Segment 2 (Operant Design).
  5. Groups post their revised behavior modification plan as a card (text or image).
  6. During the gallery walk, students click on two other groups' cards and leave a comment (question or improvement suggestion).
  7. For the Closure reflection, create a second column titled "Take-Away Words".
🙋Poll Everywhere

Purpose: Quick classroom consensus checks; real-time misconception identification.

How to use in this lesson ▼
  1. Register at polleverywhere.com.
  2. Create a Multiple Choice activity: "Is this classical or operant conditioning?"
  3. Present a scenario on the slide; students respond via pollev.com/[yourname].
  4. Results update live — use to spark discussion when the class is split.
  5. Ideal for the 5–8 min AI example thumbs-up/down activity in Section 3a.
🃏Quizlet

Purpose: Peer-driven flashcard practice for classical conditioning vocabulary.

How to use in this lesson ▼
  1. Create a set at quizlet.com with 10 terms: US, UR, NS, CS, CR, acquisition, extinction, generalization, discrimination, spontaneous recovery.
  2. Share the set link via Moodle before class.
  3. During Segment 1 (15–25 min), pairs open Quizlet on one device.
  4. Partner A reads the term; Partner B defines it without looking.
  5. Swap roles after 5 cards. Use Learn mode for self-paced review.

✅6.5 Instructor Preparation Checklist
  • ☐ Prepare AI prompts for all activities (pre-class journal, group critique, Segment 4 synthesis)
  • ☐ Test AI tools in advance; create backup prompts (in case of internet/technical failure)
  • ☐ Create an "AI Troubleshooting Station" sign for instructor desk
  • ☐ Print worksheets: AI Critique Sheet, Operant Design Worksheet, Synthesis Project Brief (≥90 copies)
  • ☐ Set up Moodle page with all resources and submission links
  • ☐ Pre-assign groups (mixed ability, mixed cultural backgrounds, ≥1 AI-confident student per group)
  • ☐ Prepare sticky notes, poster paper, markers, and name tents
  • ☐ Set up Padlet and Mentimeter presentations
  • ☐ Verify all video captions are accurate; correct any AI-generated caption errors

👥7.3 Inclusive Group Formation Principles
  • Mixed abilities — ELL + strong English speakers together
  • Mixed cultural backgrounds — to enrich discussion of diverse examples
  • Balanced confidence levels across groups
  • At least one student familiar with AI tools per group (identified from pre-class survey)

🤖8.2 AI Integration Reflection Questions
Did AI enhance or distract from learning? ▼

Data source: Exit ticket Q3; AI Reflection discussion; AI Transparency Statements.

Instructor prompt: "What percentage of students found AI helpful? Where did AI create confusion or frustration? What does this suggest about the optimal level of AI integration?"

Were students able to maintain independent reasoning? ▼

Data source: AI Transparency Statements; group discussion observations.

Instructor prompt: "Did AI use replace critical thinking, or scaffold it? Did students demonstrate effective human override? What patterns in student decision-making emerged?"

Where did students struggle with AI criticality? ▼

Data source: AI critique worksheets; AI journal entries.

Instructor prompt: "Which aspects of AI evaluation were most difficult? Did students get trapped by 'authority bias'? What scaffolding could be added next time?"

How did AI support specific groups? ▼

Data source: AI-supported differentiation observations; ELL student performance vs. prior cohorts.

Instructor prompt: "Did AI accommodations effectively support ELL/bilingual learners? Where might AI have created additional equity concerns?"

📊8.3 AI Integration Effectiveness Rubric
LevelDescriptionAction Plan
Excellent AI tasks consistently provoke critical thinking; students demonstrate sophisticated human override; integration is natural Maintain current approach
Good AI tasks useful but some students defer to AI without critique; instructor facilitates significant judgment prompting Increase explicit guidance on independent reasoning; add optional AI-focused extensions
Developing AI integration superficial; students treat AI as authoritative; tasks don't meaningfully enhance learning Revise AI prompts; model explicit human judgment; reduce AI usage where unclear

💬9.2 TA Facilitation Prompts for AI Activities

Use these prompts while circulating during AI group tasks:

  • "What did you ask the AI? Why did you phrase your prompt that way?"
  • "What do you think the AI got right? What might it have missed?"
  • "How would YOU have said it differently?"
  • "If a psychologist wrote this explanation, how might it differ from the AI's?"
  • "Are you confident trusting this? Where's your evidence?"
