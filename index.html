Teacher Education · Critical Inquiry Seminar

Teacher Professionalism in Local and International Contexts

This Critical Inquiry Seminar treats teacher professionalism as a contested idea rather than a fixed checklist. Across 150 minutes, 90 first-year students interrogate competing conceptions, compare local and international frameworks, and build an evidence-based position on how professionalism should be understood and enacted across contexts. AI integration was not requested for this lesson, so the core design is deliberately human-led; the AI panels below are an optional critical-evaluation extension only.

🤖 Design principle

This seminar was designed without AI integration. Where the optional AI Critique Lab, chat simulation or decision log are used, the stance is: AI output is a claim to be interrogated, never evidence. Students check every claim against the set readings, mark what they accept, modify or reject, and record their reasoning and source.

Section 2

Pre-Class Preparation

Before class, students read five international sources and one local policy excerpt, then complete a six-item LMS diagnostic quiz. The quiz surfaces the misconceptions that the seminar explicitly revisits.

📚 Required preparation
  • Read the OECD TALIS programme overview, focusing on what it measures about teacher autonomy, collaboration and self-efficacy.
  • Read the UNESCO Teachers page, noting how teacher professionalism is framed in global policy.
  • Browse Education International's policy and campaign material on the status, rights and professionalism of teachers.
  • Read the AITSL Australian Professional Standards for Teachers (all seven standards at a glance).
  • Read the Teaching Council of Ireland Code of Professional Conduct.
  • Read the instructor-selected local teacher standards or ministry policy excerpt for your jurisdiction and annotate one assumption it makes about teachers.
  • Complete the six-item diagnostic pre-test in your LMS before class (allow 10 minutes).
✅ Pre-class checklist
Tool Run the diagnostic pre-test in your LMS and set up Mentimeter, Padlet and Google Forms (Blackboard / Moodle / Canvas)
  1. In your LMS (Moodle, Canvas or Blackboard), create a quiz titled 'Teacher Professionalism: Diagnostic Pre-Test' and enter the six multiple-choice items from this plan. Purpose: surface prior conceptions before inquiry. Objective: ILO 1.
  2. Set one attempt, no time limit, immediate per-item feedback, and hide the score until submission so that students read the explanations. This turns the quiz into a learning task rather than a ranking.
  3. Open the quiz 48 hours before class and close it at the start of the session; export the item-level report as a CSV for the misconception review.
  4. Group the six items by the three misconception categories (core concepts, application, contextual critique), identify the two most-missed items, and use those to script the 8-minute review.
  5. In Mentimeter, create a poll titled 'Professionalism Tensions' with the options Autonomy, Accountability, Ethics and Collaboration, plus a second open-ended slide 'It depends — explain'. Purpose: make the contested framing visible in one minute. Objective: ILO 1.
  6. Set the Mentimeter poll to one vote per device, hide results until all votes are cast, then reveal them and ask two students to defend a choice they did not make. This rehearses perspective-taking before group work.
  7. Add a Mentimeter word-cloud or ranking slide asking 'Which dimension is most neglected in your local context?' Purpose: connect the concept map to students' own settings. Objective: ILO 2.
  8. In Padlet, create a wall titled 'Evidence Wall — Teacher Professionalism' with five columns: Claim, Evidence, Assumption, Context, Equity. Purpose: capture group CEACE analysis in public view. Objective: ILOs 2 and 3.
  9. Set the Padlet to 'can write' with comments enabled and a profanity filter, share the link and QR code on the projector, and assign one column per group role (evidence tracker, equity analyst, note-taker).
  10. Create a Google Form exit ticket with three required questions: one concept you now understand better, one local-international tension still unresolved, and one question you would ask a policymaker. Purpose: capture learning and remaining tension. Objective: ILO 4.
  11. In a shared Google Drive folder, create one Google Doc per group from a CEACE template with five headings and a Compass section with four quadrants; share the folder with view-comment rights. Purpose: scaffold group synthesis. Objective: ILOs 3 and 4.
  12. Test every link, QR code and projector view 24 hours before class, and print a low-tech backup packet (readings, CEACE template, exit ticket) for students without devices.

Section 3

Teaching & Learning Activities

The run sheet moves from a provocative hook (20 min) — 'A local teacher is praised for high test scores but criticized for not adapting to diverse learners; an international report ranks the system highly. Is this teacher professional?' — through a modelled concept map, revisited to show how the dimensions shift across local and international policy contexts, and a think-pair-share on the most valued and most neglected dimensions (15 min), CEACE case analysis in 15 groups (30 min), a jigsaw exchange and Padlet evidence wall (20 min), a structured debate on the motion 'Teacher professionalism is best strengthened by external accountability standards rather than professional autonomy' with a mid-debate position switch and a contextual-nuance requirement (25 min), and Compass position-building across autonomy, accountability, ethics and equity, and collaboration and context (15 min), to synthesis and closure (25 min). Guiding questions run throughout: Whose definition of professionalism is promoted, and whose voice is missing? What assumptions about teachers and accountability underlie this framework? How do local histories and policy borrowing shape expectations? What are the equity implications for teachers and learners? When does accountability support professionalism, and when does it undermine it?

1 Round 1 — CEACE case analysis and jigsaw exchange

Groups of five to six (15 groups) receive a case packet with four short excerpts: a local standards or ministry policy excerpt, an OECD TALIS indicator on autonomy or collaboration, a UNESCO teacher-policy excerpt, and a news article on accountability, identity or equity. The instructor models the CEACE protocol — Claim, Evidence, Assumption, Context, Equity — on the first excerpt (5 min); groups complete a CEACE chart for their assigned excerpt (20 min); the class then regroups into jigsaw groups, where each expert explains their excerpt and its CEACE analysis to peers from other groups and the group records one claim, one assumption and one equity implication on the Padlet Evidence Wall (20 min).

🎓 Instructor guidance on critical AI use
  • Model the CEACE protocol aloud with the local excerpt before groups begin, and make your own assumptions visible while modelling.
  • Protect the local-international balance: do not let either framing become the unmarked 'normal' against which the other is judged.
  • Circulate with two prompts only — 'What is the evidence for that?' and 'Who is missing from this claim?' — to keep inquiry open rather than steering answers.
  • Name misconceptions when you hear them (compliance equals professionalism, standards are neutral, autonomy and accountability are opposites) and ask the group to test them against the case packet.
  • Record the language students use about teachers, accountability and equity; it is data for the next session and for your own reflection.
  • If you use the optional AI panels, require students to log every accept/modify/reject decision with a reason and an authoritative source, and treat unverified AI claims as inadmissible evidence.

Section 4

Assessment Methods

Formative assessment is embedded in inquiry and observation; summative assessment is a Critical Inquiry Portfolio of comparative analysis, a position paper and a reflective memo, marked against a six-criterion rubric: critical analysis of professionalism concepts, contextual comparison, evidence and source evaluation, argumentation and position, reflexivity and implications, and collaboration and inquiry process.

🎯 Post-test 6 items

Six items mirror the pre-test's three misconception categories — core concepts, application to cases and contextual critique — so growth can be compared item by item.

Section 7

Differentiation & Inclusivity

The seminar offers multiple entry points — visual, textual, oral and written — with structured roles, sentence frames and accessible materials so that all 90 students can participate in critical inquiry.

♿ Support
  • Multiple entry points: visual concept map, policy text, oral debate or written reflection.
  • Group roles — facilitator, evidence tracker, equity analyst, note-taker and presenter — rotated during the jigsaw to structure participation.
  • Sentence frames for dialogue and writing: 'The evidence suggests… However, this assumption may overlook… A more context-sensitive view would…'
  • Pre-teach key terms — autonomy, accountability, professional identity and policy borrowing — with local examples and a glossary.
  • Accessibility: captioned videos, large-print case packets, translated key terms, a quiet reflection option and flexible grouping.
  • Provide the CEACE template as a printed grid and as a shared Google Doc for students who prefer typing or need extra time.
  • Publish the case packet and Padlet link 48 hours in advance so students with caring or work commitments can prepare.
🚀 Extension
  • Write a 1,000-word comparative policy brief comparing the local standards with one international framework.
  • Lead a Socratic seminar on the debate motion in a later session, using the case packet as evidence.
  • Analyze an additional framework (for example the GTCS Standards for Registration in Scotland or a Canadian provincial standard) and present a critique.
  • Audit the case packet for missing voices and propose a fifth excerpt that centres equity and student perspective.

Section 8

Reflection & Improvement

Success is judged by the quality of student questioning, evidence use, perspective-taking and equity analysis — not by how quickly groups agree. The closing reflection asks: Which assumption about teacher professionalism was most challenged today? How did comparing local and international contexts change your view? What evidence would you need before making a policy recommendation? The session ends by previewing the next topic — teacher evaluation, professional development or teacher identity. Instructors also gather a short student survey ('Did the Critical Inquiry Seminar help you analyze local and international contexts? What would improve the inquiry process?') and review which misconceptions persisted. Modification strategies for the next iteration: update local policy examples each semester, adjust group size and timing for large classes, add evidence-analysis scaffolding, and revise the debate motion to reflect current local and international policy debates.
