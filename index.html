Educational Psychology · Learning Theories

Introduction to Learning Theories

This 150-minute flipped lesson introduces first-year undergraduates to the five major learning theories — behaviorism, cognitivism, constructivism, social constructivism and connectivism — and to the AI literacy needed to judge machine-generated explanations of them. AI is positioned throughout as a cognitive partner to be interrogated, never an authority to be trusted: students learn to accept, modify or reject AI output with reasons.

⏱ 150 minutes 👥 90 students 🎓 First-year undergraduates 🌐 English

Section 1

Intended Learning Outcomes

Each outcome is engineered so that AI integration is not an add-on but part of what success looks like — including the judgement to question AI output.

ILODescription🤖 AI Integration
ILO1Analyse the core assumptions of behaviorism, cognitivism, constructivism, social constructivism and connectivism by comparing how each theory explains learning.Students compare an AI summary of each theory against the reading and flag where the AI conflates, flattens or omits assumptions.
ILO2Evaluate the applicability of learning theories to authentic educational scenarios through collaborative dialogue and evidence-based reasoning.AI is consulted for candidate solutions to a scenario; every AI suggestion must be justified with theoretical evidence or set aside.
ILO3Create a group concept map or mini-lesson design that applies one learning theory to a real-world learning problem, justifying design choices with theoretical principles.AI acts as a design consultant; students document which AI ideas shaped the artefact and where human judgement overrode them.
ILO4Critically evaluate AI-generated explanations of learning theories for accuracy, completeness, bias and pedagogical limitations, and decide when to accept, modify or reject AI outputs.Directly assessed in the AI Critique Lab: students cast accept / modify / reject verdicts on every AI claim and justify each verdict.
ILO5Document and reflect on independent reasoning and collaborative knowledge-building when using AI as a cognitive partner.The AI decision log and the final reflection prompts capture where AI supported thinking and where it had to be overridden.
🤖 Design principle

AI-generated content is treated as an object of analysis, never an authority. For every AI claim students must state whether they accepted, modified or rejected it, and why human judgement did or did not take precedence.

Pre-Class Preparation

Before the session students watch short explainers, complete a diagnostic pre-test that tags misconceptions, and interrogate one AI explanation of a learning theory of their choice.

📚 Required preparation
  • Read the Simply Psychology overview: <a href="https://www.simplypsychology.org/learning-theories.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Learning Theories</a>.
  • Watch Khan Academy's explainer on <a href="https://www.khanacademy.org/test-prep/mcat/behavior/learning-and-memory/v/classical-conditioning" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Classical Conditioning</a>.
  • Watch Crash Course Psychology: <a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8nz2dtv--ok" target="_blank" rel="noopener">How We Learn</a> (captions available).
  • Complete the 8-item diagnostic pre-test before class — it tags your misconceptions for in-class review.
  • Complete the pre-class AI exploration task: prompt a chatbot to explain one learning theory and record what it explained well, what it oversimplified, omitted or biased, and one question you would ask a human expert to verify it.
  • Bring one AI-generated claim to class for collective critique.
  • Reflect on the guiding questions: How does social interaction shape what counts as learning? How does each theory position the learner, the teacher and knowledge? What evidence would convince you that one theory explains a situation better than another?

Section 3

Teaching & Learning Activities

A three-phase run sheet (20 + 110 + 20 minutes) that moves from a provocative hook, through a chunked interactive lecture, a jigsaw, an AI Critique Lab and case-based concept mapping, to a synthesis that mirrors the pre-test misconceptions.

1 Round 1 — Jigsaw — co-constructing theory expertise

In 30 minutes, the 90 students form 15 expert groups of 6 (three groups per learning theory). Each group analyses one theory using the guiding questions and prepares a 3-minute peer-teaching summary. Groups then reshuffle so every new group contains one expert per theory, and each expert teaches their theory to peers. Knowledge is co-constructed through dialogue and distributed expertise — the social-constructivist core of the lesson. Record your accept / modify / reject decisions about any AI help you use.

🎓 Instructor guidance on critical AI use
  • Use AI as a consultant, not an oracle: 'ask the AI, then check it.'
  • Recognise when AI is appropriate versus when human judgement is essential.
  • Avoid unreflective reliance: 'If you cannot explain it without AI, you do not yet own it.'
  • Require every group to log each AI suggestion as accepted, modified or rejected, with a reason.
  • Circulate during the AI tasks and probe: 'What did the AI leave out, and how do you know?'

Assessment Methods

Formative assessment makes critical AI engagement visible and coachable; summative assessment pairs a co-constructed group artefact with an individual reflection on independent judgement.

Section 7

Differentiation & Inclusivity

Multiple entry points through group roles, scaffolds for struggling learners, extensions for advanced learners, and AI-supported accessibility.

♿ Support
  • Assign rotating group roles (facilitator, recorder, skeptic, AI critic) so every student has a clear entry point.
  • Peer mentoring within jigsaw groups: stronger students model peer-teaching for others.
  • AI tools provide simplified, step-by-step explanations for students who need extra scaffolding.
  • Accessibility: AI-generated captions, text-to-speech and translation for the pre-class videos.
  • Tiered guidance on AI use, written to accommodate students with very different levels of AI familiarity.
  • Tactile and kinesthetic alternatives (printed scenario cards, sticky notes) for the concept-mapping task.
🚀 Extension
  • Design a cross-theory integration model that combines two or more theories to explain one classroom problem.
  • Lead a calibration session in which the class evaluates the quality of AI-generated feedback together.
  • Use AI to explore a complex extension (for example connectivism in networked learning) and then critique how the AI framed it.
