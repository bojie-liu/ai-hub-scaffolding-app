Teacher Professionalism in Local and International Contexts

This 150-minute critical inquiry seminar positions teacher professionalism as a contested, context-dependent construct rather than a fixed checklist of traits. You will interrogate four competing discourses — trait-based, managerial, democratic and activist — compare how professionalism is framed in your own jurisdiction and in a contrasting international context, and test whether "global best practice" can travel. AI is integrated throughout as an object of critique, not an oracle: you will use it to generate comparative accounts, then systematically test those accounts against authoritative sources, identify bias and hallucination, and document where human judgement overrode the machine.

🧭 The stance this lesson takes

Professionalism is treated as something people argue about, not something a document settles. Every claim you meet — from a ministry, a union, a code of conduct or an AI assistant — is read as a positioned account with interests behind it. Your job is to surface those interests, weigh the evidence, and be willing to move.

Section 1

Intended Learning Outcomes

Seven outcomes, each paired with the AI integration engineered into it. The first five build the disciplinary argument; outcomes 6 and 7 make critical AI literacy an assessable outcome in its own right rather than a bolt-on.

ILODescription🤖 AI Integration
ILO 1 Analyse competing conceptualisations of teacher professionalism (trait-based, managerial, democratic, activist) and surface the assumptions and interests underpinning each. (Bloom's: Analyze · Critical Inquiry: assumption-surfacing) In the introduction, students critique an AI-generated paragraph and identify which discourse the AI implicitly assumes; the AI Critique Lab makes that assumption-surfacing explicit.
ILO 2 Compare and evaluate how teacher professionalism is framed in the local jurisdiction and in at least one contrasting international context, using policy documents, codes of conduct and professional standards as evidence. (Bloom's: Evaluate · Critical Inquiry: evidence appraisal) AI generates the initial comparison in Segment B; students then verify every claim against a named, human-authored source before it can enter their argument.
ILO 3 Critique the transferability of "international best practice" in teacher professionalism, accounting for historical, cultural, political and economic context. (Bloom's: Evaluate · Critical Inquiry: contextual reasoning) Students identify the AI's Anglocentric bias, its over-generalisation and its erasure of genuine contextual conflict in the comparative task.
ILO 4 Construct and defend a reasoned, evidence-based position on a contested issue in teacher professionalism within structured seminar dialogue, and revise that position in response to counter-arguments. (Bloom's: Create · Critical Inquiry: dialogic position defence) The portfolio reflective commentary asks where and why human judgement took precedence over an AI recommendation in building that position.
ILO 5 Collaborate in an inquiry community to interrogate evidence, challenge peers' reasoning constructively, and co-build a shared claim with warrants. (Bloom's: Analyze / Evaluate · Social Constructivist element) Groups jointly interrogate AI output and must negotiate a shared verdict, so the critique is a collective product rather than a private opinion.
ILO 6 Evaluate AI-generated accounts of teacher professionalism for accuracy, completeness, recency, cultural bias and hallucinated evidence — identifying at least two specific errors, omissions or Anglocentric framings. (AI literacy: critical evaluation of AI outputs) Core AI outcome Assessed directly through the Critical AI Engagement Log and Portfolio Part B; at least one hallucination, bias or omission must be documented.
ILO 7 Exercise independent judgement in deciding when, whether and how to act on AI-generated content, documenting what was accepted, modified or rejected and articulating where human expertise and contextual knowledge took precedence. (AI literacy: independent judgement and transparency) Core AI outcome Assessed through the AI transparency note in Segment D, the Feedback Interrogation Sheet and the "Critical AI Engagement" rubric criterion.
🤖 Design principle

AI output is treated as a hypothesis to be tested, never a source to be cited. Every AI-derived claim must be verified against a named, human-authored source before it enters your argument. The lesson deliberately engineers situations in which the AI is fluent, confident and wrong — because fluency is a stylistic property, not evidence of correctness.

🧠 Cognitive range

The session moves from analyse (ILO 1) through evaluate (ILOs 2–3, 6) to create (ILO 4) — a deliberate progression from describing discourses to defending a position.

🗣️ Inquiry spine

Assumption-surfacing → evidence appraisal → contextual reasoning → dialogic position defence. The same spine recurs in every segment, so the intellectual moves become habitual.

🤝 Social dimension

ILO 5 is assessed formatively through the observation protocol and peer checklist, not in the individual portfolio — preserving individual accountability while valuing collaboration.

🏷️ Collaborative annotation task Hypothesis

  • [ASSUMPTION] — an unstated premise
  • [EVIDENCE] — an empirical or documentary warrant
  • [CONTESTED] — a claim you believe is arguable
  • [CONTEXT] — a claim that may not travel across contexts

Open Hypothesis collaborative annotation and follow the numbered steps below.

🔎 Guiding inquiry questions
  1. Who gets to define what counts as "professional" behaviour for teachers — the state, the profession, the market, or the community?
  2. Whose interests are served by each definition?
  3. What evidence would change your mind about whether teaching is a profession?
  4. What is lost when a definition of professionalism travels from one context to another?

Keep these questions open as you read — the tribunal in Segment C will return to them.

Explain what makes teaching a profession, and compare how teacher professionalism is understood in [your local jurisdiction] and in Finland. Cite sources.

Complete the observation log:

  • Paste the AI's response.
  • Identify one claim you believe is accurate and explain how you would verify it.
  • Identify one claim that is inaccurate, oversimplified or culturally biased, and explain why.

Section 3

Teaching & Learning Activities

150 minutes total — Introduction 20 min (13%) · Development 105 min (70%) · Synthesis and closure 25 min (17%). AI touchpoints are marked throughout.

🎬 Introduction · 20 minutes

Hook — "The Professional or the Bureaucrat?" 5 min

Two contrasting artefacts are displayed side by side.

📜 Artefact A — UNESCO/ILO (1966)

An extract affirming that teachers should enjoy academic freedom and that teaching should be recognised as a profession exercising professional judgement in the interests of students and society.

📋 Artefact B — performance-management policy

An extract from a contemporary policy specifying lesson-plan templates, scripted curricula and test-score targets tied to appraisal.

Ask: "Both claim to be about teacher professionalism. Can both be right?" Students vote via Mentimeter before any discussion. The split is recorded and revisited at closure.

Pre-test review with misconception analysis 6 min

Display the aggregate pre-test results. Explicitly name the three target misconceptions and ask students to locate where in their readings each is challenged. Use a think-pair-share (60 seconds each) before a whole-class debrief.

  • (a) professionalism = compliance with standards
  • (b) "best practice" is universally transferable
  • (c) professionalism is a fixed, ahistorical trait list

AI-generated claim critique — modelled critical questioning 6 min

The instructor displays a pre-screened AI-generated paragraph on teacher professionalism containing two deliberate flaws: a hallucinated citation and an Anglocentric generalisation (for example, "all high-performing systems now require teachers to hold a master's degree"). The instructor then models the critical questioning ladder aloud:

RungQuestion
1What is the claim?
2What evidence is offered — and does that evidence exist?
3Whose context is being generalised from?
4What is missing?
5What would I need to check before using this?

Students then apply the ladder in pairs to a second AI-generated sentence. This models the exact protocol used in Segment B.

Real-world connection and roadmap 3 min

Frame the stakes: teacher shortages, migration of teachers across borders, debates over professional registration, and the global circulation of "what works" in education. Preview the session arc: map the terrain → compare contexts with AI → tribunal → position statement.

A Segment A — Mapping the contested terrain · 25 minutes

Interactive mini-lecture with think-pair-share 10 min

Present the four discourses using an animated concept map. Pause after each discourse for a 60-second think-pair-share: "Which discourse does your own schooling experience most resemble?"

Group card-sort task 10 min

Each of the 15 groups receives a set of 12 statement cards drawn verbatim from real codes, standards and policy documents (anonymised). Groups classify each card into one of the four discourses and mark any card that is contested — that is, a card that could plausibly belong to two discourses. Each group must justify one contested classification to a neighbouring group.

Assumption archaeology within Segment A

Each group receives one policy extract and answers four questions in writing, then exchanges extracts with a neighbouring group and compares assumptions.

🔍 The four questions
  1. What does this document assume a teacher is?
  2. What does it assume a teacher should be trusted to do?
  3. Whose voice is absent from this document?
  4. What would a teacher in a very different context find strange about it?
📊 Gamified poll Mentimeter · 5 min

Poll: "Which discourse best describes teacher professionalism in your local context today?" Live results are displayed. The instructor facilitates a brief discussion of divergence — particularly where students from different school backgrounds disagree. This divergence is itself the lesson: professionalism is experienced differently across settings.

B Segment B — AI-enabled comparative inquiry: local vs international · 35 minutes

Structured AI interaction task 20 min

Groups of six work in three sub-pairs, each with a distinct AI prompt.

1 Pair 1 — Local framing
Summarise how teacher professionalism is defined and regulated in [local jurisdiction]. Include the relevant code of conduct or professional standards and cite your sources.
2 Pair 2 — International framing

Compare teacher professionalism in [local jurisdiction] and Finland. What are the three most important differences? Cite sources.

📌 Requirement

Each group must document at least three verdicts, including at least one rejection and at least one hallucinated or unverifiable citation.

AI verdict negotiation within Segment B

The full group compares the three AI outputs and discovers that the AI produces inconsistent claims depending on how the question is framed — a powerful demonstration that AI output is prompt-dependent and not authoritative. Groups document the inconsistencies in their log and discuss: "If the AI says different things depending on how we ask, what does that tell us about using it as a source?"

Instructor mini-briefing on AI limitations 5 min

  • Hallucination: AI generates plausible but non-existent citations and statistics. Always verify.
  • Anglocentric and WEIRD bias: training data over-represents English-language, high-income, Western sources. "International" often means "US, UK, Australia".
  • Averaging: AI tends to produce a smooth consensus that erases genuine contextual conflict.
  • Recency: policy changes fast; AI knowledge has a cutoff.
  • Authority laundering: fluent AI prose can feel authoritative even when unsupported.
🤖 Explicit rule for the session

AI output is a hypothesis to be tested, never a source to be cited. Every AI-derived claim must be verified against a named, human-authored source before it enters your argument.

Cross-check against the Source Pack 10 min

Groups verify their AI claims against the provided Source Pack (printed and digital): the UNESCO/ILO (1966) Recommendation, OECD TALIS indicators, the local code of conduct, and one contrasting national standards document. Groups annotate the pack in the margin: "AI said X; source says Y."

C Segment C — Critical Inquiry Seminar: The Professionalism Tribunal · 30 minutes
⚖️ Motion

"This house believes that teacher professionalism is best secured through external accountability standards rather than professional self-regulation."

Stakeholder role allocation

One role per group, with two groups sharing the largest roles.

🏛️ 1 · Ministry of Education

State regulator: defends external standards and public accountability.

✊ 2 · Teachers' union / professional body

Defends self-regulation, professional judgement and collective standards.

🏫 3 · School leaders / principals

Caught between compliance and the conditions for good teaching.

👪 4 · Parents and community

Asks what transparency and trust mean for families and students.

🎓 5 · Teacher educators and researchers

Brings evidence on standards, learning and policy transfer.

🤖 6 · "The AI"

Must defend an AI-generated position on the motion, then publicly concede where the AI's reasoning fails under cross-examination — a deliberately designed critical AI literacy role.

Format

Opening statements · 18 min
3 minutes per role, grounded in the Source Pack.
Structured cross-examination · 7 min
Each role poses one question to another role.
Free debate · 5 min
Instructor-facilitated, using the critical questioning ladder.

Instructor facilitation moves

  • "What is your evidence, and how do you know it?"
  • "Whose interests does that definition serve?"
  • "What would have to be true for the opposing view to be correct?"
  • "Is that claim context-specific or general?"

Students may, as an accessibility accommodation, submit a written tribunal statement in place of an oral opening statement.

Position-shift vote

Students re-vote on the motion. The instructor highlights shifts and asks students to name the argument that moved them — making position revision an explicit, valued outcome rather than a failure of consistency.

D Segment D — Position statement drafting · 15 minutes Gradual release

Individually, draft a 150-word position statement on the tribunal motion. The instructor first models one aloud, thinking through the choice of evidence and the AI transparency note; you then draft your own using the structured template.

Requirements

  • At least one piece of evidence from a local source and one from an international source.
  • At least one counter-argument acknowledged and rebutted.
  • An explicit AI transparency note: "One AI-generated claim I [accepted / modified / rejected] was ___, because ___."

This draft feeds directly into the summative portfolio (Part C).

Sentence starters

"My claim is ___ because the evidence from ___ suggests ___" · "The strongest objection is ___, but this fails to account for ___" · "One AI-generated claim I rejected was ___, because the source said ___" · "I want to challenge that because ___"

Peers exchange drafts for a two-minute "evidence check" before submission.

🎓 Instructor guidance on critical AI use
  • Frame AI as evidence to examine, not an authority to copy. State the session rule aloud: AI output is a hypothesis to be tested, never a source to be cited.
  • Model the questioning ladder before you ask students to use it. Students imitate the intellectual moves they see, not the ones they are told about.
  • Require at least one rejection. A log in which every AI claim is "Accepted" is treated as evidence of unreflective reliance, not of a good AI.
  • Name the failure modes explicitly — hallucination, Anglocentric bias, averaging, recency, authority laundering — so students have vocabulary for what they notice.
  • Reward position revision. Publicly value students who change their mind with reasons; otherwise the tribunal rewards performance rather than inquiry.
  • Keep human judgement sovereign. Every AI-derived claim must be traced to a named, human-authored source before it enters an argument.

Section 4

Assessment Methods

Formative assessment runs throughout the seminar; summative assessment is a single comparative inquiry portfolio with an explicit critical AI engagement criterion.

🎯 Post-test 10 items

The post-test mirrors the pre-test misconception categories — discourses of professionalism, comparative and contextual reasoning, and critical AI literacy — and adds two transfer items: item 9 (apply) asks you to analyse a scripted-lesson-plan policy through the four discourses, and item 10 (evaluate) asks why Finnish approaches might not transfer to your local context.

👀 Formative assessment during class

Critical Inquiry Observation Protocol

The instructor and two teaching assistants circulate with a structured observation sheet capturing:

  • Quality of evidence use — does the student cite sources or assert opinions?
  • Quality of questioning — does the student probe assumptions?
  • Willingness to revise a position in light of counter-evidence.
  • Distribution of participation across group members.

Real-time polling and exit ticket

  • Mentimeter polls at three points: opening vote, discourse poll, position-shift vote.
  • Exit ticket (3-2-1): 3 claims you can now defend with evidence · 2 tensions you still see · 1 question you want to pursue.

Peer-assessment checklist with calibration

Before the tribunal, the class calibrates on a 30-second anonymised video clip of a seminar exchange, scoring it against a four-item checklist — evidence, reasoning, responsiveness, clarity. This calibration ensures inter-rater consistency before peer scoring begins.

AI-supported formative checks

  • Students submit their AI-generated comparison alongside their annotated critique and Critical AI Engagement Log.
  • The instructor reviews the logs specifically for evidence of critical engagement versus unreflective reliance — a log where every AI claim is "Accepted" with no verification triggers a targeted check-in.
  • Spot-check: the instructor selects three AI claims from across the cohort and asks the class to verify them live in the following session.

Collaboration quality (ILO 5) is assessed formatively through the observation protocol and peer checklist, not in the individual portfolio, to preserve individual accountability.

🤖 AI-supported feedback and learning support
Stage 1 — AI-generated formative feedback
Students submit their Part C draft to a configured AI feedback tool — a rubric-loaded custom GPT, or an LMS-integrated assistant such as Gradescope or Turnitin Revision Assistant. The tool returns criterion-referenced comments on argument structure, evidence use and clarity.
Stage 2 — Instructor review and override (48-hour window)
Before any AI feedback is released to students, the instructor reviews it, flags inaccurate or generic comments and adds human annotations. AI feedback is never released unreviewed.
Stage 3 — Student interrogation of AI feedback
Students complete a Feedback Interrogation Sheet: which comments do you agree with, and why; which do you disagree with, and what evidence supports your disagreement; what did the AI miss that a human reader would notice; which comments will you act on, and which will you override — with rationale.
Stage 4 — Calibration session (Week 5, 20 min)
Instructor and students jointly evaluate three anonymised samples: one with AI feedback only, one with instructor feedback only, one with both. The class discusses where AI feedback was accurate, where it was generic or wrong, and what that reveals about the limits of automated assessment.
Stage 5 — Human judgement prevails
All summative grades are determined by human assessors. AI feedback supplements — never replaces — instructor pedagogical judgement. Students who override AI feedback with documented rationale are explicitly rewarded under the "Critical AI Engagement" rubric criterion.
Tool Configure a rubric-loaded AI feedback assistant (Stage 1)

Purpose: to provide fast criterion-referenced formative feedback that students then interrogate, rather than to grade. Learning objective: ILO 7 — exercising independent judgement about when and how to act on AI output.

  1. Create a custom GPT (or an LMS-integrated assistant) and paste the six summative rubric criteria from the table above into its instructions.
  2. Add an instruction that the assistant must give feedback only against the criteria, must quote the student's own words, and must never invent sources.
  3. Add a second instruction requiring the assistant to state, for each comment, whether it is about argument structure, evidence use or clarity.
  4. Test the assistant on three sample drafts and note where it produces generic or inaccurate comments — these become the examples you use in the Week 5 calibration session.
  5. Set up an instructor review queue so that no AI comment reaches a student before you have annotated or removed it (Stage 2).
  6. Distribute the Feedback Interrogation Sheet alongside the released feedback and require students to complete it before acting on any comment.
  7. Record every student override, with rationale, and reward defensible overrides under the "Critical AI Engagement" criterion.
Tool Use Kahoot as a low-stakes misconceptions check

Purpose: to re-test the three target misconceptions competitively and quickly, and to show the class its own error pattern in real time. Learning objective: consolidates ILO 1 by making the misconceptions visible a second time, after teaching.

  1. Sign in at Kahoot and create a new quiz titled "Professionalism: misconception check".
  2. Add six True/False or Multiple choice questions built directly from the pre-test misconception targets.
  3. Include one deliberately tricky item about policy transfer to prompt debate, for example "If a policy works in Finland it will work here."
  4. Set the timer to 20 seconds per question and enable the "show the class answer distribution" option.
  5. Host in Classic mode or assign Challenge mode if you want it completed asynchronously.
  6. After each question, pause and ask one student to justify the correct answer with a source — the discussion, not the score, is the point.
  7. Share the report with the class and compare the error pattern with the pre-test aggregate.

Section 7

Differentiation & Inclusivity

Multiple entry points, tiered inquiry tasks, sentence starters and an AI Use Ladder so that a cohort of 90 with very different confidence levels can all meet the same critical standard.

♿ Support
  • Multiple entry points: text-based (readings), visual (concept maps, card sorts), oral (tribunal), written (position statement) — students may demonstrate inquiry competence through any combination.
  • Structured sentence starters for seminar dialogue: "My claim is ___ because the evidence from ___ suggests ___"; "I want to challenge that because ___".
  • Quiet preparation space available for students who need to draft before speaking.
  • Alternative to public speaking: students may submit a written tribunal statement in place of an oral opening statement.
  • All PDFs screen-reader accessible and tagged; large-print and high-contrast versions of card-sort materials; captioned video materials.
  • Extended time on the pre-test and post-test for students with registered accommodations.
🚀 Extension
  • Lead a group's AI critique and present the group's verdicts to the class.
  • Investigate teacher migration and the international recognition of qualifications as a professionalism issue.
  • Explore decolonising critiques of "global" professionalism frameworks.
  • Design a comparative rubric for evaluating national professional standards.
  • Tier 3 inquiry task: critique the epistemological assumptions of the comparative method itself; examine teacher professionalism in a low-income or post-colonial context.

🤖 AI Use Ladder and AI-supported differentiation

Students self-select a level; the minimum requirement for the portfolio is Level 2. This ensures students with low AI confidence are not disadvantaged while maintaining the critical engagement standard.

1 Level 1 — no AI

Use the Source Pack only. The critical reading task is identical; only the object of critique changes.

2 Level 2 — AI for brainstorming

Use AI to generate ideas, but verify everything against named sources. Full log required for AI-derived claims.

3 Level 3 — AI for comparative drafting

Use AI to draft the comparison, with a full critical log documenting every accept, modify and reject decision.

  • Scaffolding for struggling learners: AI generates simplified explanations of Sachs's four discourses; students must check the simplification against the original text and note what was lost.
  • Extension for advanced learners: AI generates comparative accounts of teacher professionalism in under-represented contexts (for example Rwanda, Vietnam, Chile), which students then verify and critique.
  • Accessibility accommodations via AI: AI-generated captions for video materials; text-to-speech for readings; translation support for multilingual students; AI-assisted summarisation of dense policy documents (with verification required).

Section 8

Reflection & Improvement

Success indicators tied to the Critical Inquiry Seminar, student feedback instruments, AI-specific reflection data, and the modification strategies that follow from each pattern of evidence.

📈 Success indicators
  • Proportion of students who revise their position between the opening and closing votes (target: ≥40%).
  • Quality of questioning in the tribunal — the frequency of assumption-probing questions versus opinion assertions.
  • Distribution of participation across all 15 groups: no group silent, no individual dominating.
  • Quality of the Padlet claim-and-warrant statements — are warrants evident and sourced?
📝 Student feedback
  • Critical Inquiry Self-Evaluation Form (post-session, 5 min): "How well did I use evidence today? How well did I question assumptions — my own and others'? Did I change my mind, and why?"
  • Mid-semester focus group with 8–10 volunteers on the seminar format.
🤖 AI-specific reflection

Student feedback on AI integration

"Did AI tools enhance or distract from your learning? Were you able to maintain your own thinking? Which AI task was most valuable, and which felt like a distraction?"

Instructor reflection on AI effectiveness

"Did the AI integration achieve its intended goals? Where did students struggle with AI criticality? Did any students over-rely on AI despite the log requirement? Did the 'AI' tribunal role work as intended?"

Data on student critical engagement

  • Percentage of AI claims correctly identified as inaccurate, biased or hallucinated.
  • Distribution of accept / modify / reject verdicts across the cohort.
  • Number of hallucinated citations caught versus missed.
  • Correlation between Critical AI Engagement Log quality and overall portfolio performance.
  • Instances where students overrode AI feedback, and the quality of their rationale.
🔁 Modification strategies based on pedagogical theory
If you observe…Then…
Position revision rates are lowStrengthen the cross-examination structure and introduce a mid-tribunal "evidence challenge" pause, drawing on Critical Inquiry principles of structured disputation.
AI critique is superficialReplace the generic AI comparison prompt with a deliberately flawed, instructor-authored AI response containing known errors, so students practise detection before generating their own.
Group participation is unevenIntroduce assigned dialogic roles (questioner, evidence-checker, synthesiser, devil's advocate) rotating each segment, consistent with Social Constructivist role structuring.
The Source Pack is too dense for first-year studentsTier the Source Pack into "essential" and "extension" extracts, consistent with Scaffolded Instruction.
AI access is inequitableProvide a curated set of pre-generated AI transcripts for students without reliable access, preserving the critical evaluation task while removing the access barrier.
The tribunal runs over timeMove Segment D drafting to a post-class asynchronous task with a same-day submission deadline, preserving the reflective momentum.
