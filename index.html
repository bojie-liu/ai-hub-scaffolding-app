Foundations of Education · Lesson 3

Introduction to Learning Theories

This 150-minute flipped session introduces first-year undergraduates to five core learning theories - behaviourism, cognitivism, constructivism, social constructivism and humanism. Students co-construct comparative understanding through a jigsaw, peer critique and AI-assisted problem-solving, while treating every AI output as a claim to be interrogated rather than an answer to be trusted.

Section 1

Intended Learning Outcomes

Every outcome below is engineered so that AI integration is explicit: each ILO names both the cognitive demand and the critical, transparent way AI is used to support (and be questioned by) the learner.

ILODescription🤖 AI Integration
ILO1Analyse core learning theories - behaviourism, cognitivism, constructivism, social constructivism and humanism - using key concepts, evidence and theoretical assumptions. (Bloom: Analyse)Students prompt an AI for a one-paragraph summary of each theory, then audit the summary sentence by sentence for accuracy, omissions and bias against the course readings. AI offers breadth; students supply the evidence and the verdict.
ILO2Evaluate the applicability of learning theories to diverse educational contexts, learner needs and cultural settings. (Bloom: Evaluate)Groups ask an AI to suggest how a theory would play out in a specific context, then interrogate the suggestion for cultural bias and pedagogical fit before accepting, modifying or rejecting it.
ILO3Collaborate in groups to construct a shared explanatory model of learning that synthesises multiple theoretical perspectives. (Bloom: Create)AI-generated concept maps and explanations are used as raw material that groups critique, correct and re-draw; the concept-map builder records the group's own connections and marks any AI-sourced node.
ILO4Critically evaluate AI-generated explanations of learning theories for accuracy, completeness, bias and pedagogical limitations. (Bloom: Evaluate; AI literacy)The AI Critique Lab, the chat-synthesis task and the AI interaction log turn evaluation into a concrete, logged habit: identify hallucinations, oversimplifications and biased framing, and cite the source that settles the question.
ILO5Exercise independent judgement by deciding when to adopt, modify or reject AI-generated content in constructing a theory-informed learning activity. (Bloom: Evaluate/Create; AI literacy)Students document every decision - accepted, modified or rejected - with a rationale, and explain where human judgement (ethics, cultural sensitivity, pedagogical fit) took precedence over the AI's recommendation.
🤖 Design principle

AI is a starting point for interrogation, never a final authority. Every AI output in this lesson must be checked against course readings, evaluated for accuracy, completeness and bias, and then deliberately accepted, modified or rejected with a documented rationale. Human pedagogical, cultural and ethical judgement always takes precedence.

Pre-Class Preparation

Before class you complete the flipped-learning preparation: read the assigned sources, watch the captioned video, take the 8-item adaptive pre-test and finish the AI engagement task. Work through <a href='https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/6-introduction' target='_blank' rel='noopener'>OpenStax Psychology 2e, Chapter 6: Learning</a> and the <a href='https://www.simplypsychology.org/learning-theories.html' target='_blank' rel='noopener'>Simply Psychology overview of learning theories</a>, then bring your AI prompt, its output and your evaluation to class for the AI Critique Lab.

📚 Required preparation
  • Read <a href='https://openstax.org/books/psychology-2e/pages/6-introduction' target='_blank' rel='noopener'>OpenStax Psychology 2e, Chapter 6: Learning</a>, focusing on conditioning, observational learning and the shift toward cognitive accounts.
  • Skim the <a href='https://www.simplypsychology.org/learning-theories.html' target='_blank' rel='noopener'>Simply Psychology overview of learning theories</a> to meet behaviourism, cognitivism, constructivism, social constructivism and humanism side by side.
  • Watch the captioned instructor video Five Learning Theories in 12 Minutes (transcript and audio description provided) and note one idea per theory.
  • Complete the 8-item adaptive diagnostic pre-test in your LMS; the feedback directs you to the exact reading section or video timestamp for anything you miss.
  • Write a one-paragraph first answer to: how do behaviourism and constructivism differ in explaining how learning happens? What role does social interaction play, and how might cultural context shape which theory is most useful?
  • Complete the AI engagement task: prompt an AI to summarise one theory, record the exact prompt and output, evaluate it for accuracy, completeness, missing nuance and bias, then post a short reflection (what it got right, what it oversimplified, which sources you would check, how training data might bias it, and where your own judgement was needed) to the LMS discussion board.
✅ Pre-class checklist
Tool Run the 8-item adaptive diagnostic pre-test in your LMS (Moodle / Canvas / Blackboard)
  1. In your LMS (Moodle, Canvas or Blackboard) open the quiz named Learning Theories - Diagnostic Pre-test (8 items).
  2. Confirm the quiz is set to adaptive, one-question-at-a-time mode with immediate feedback so each answer unlocks a hint.
  3. Attempt all eight items without looking up the answers; the quiz is diagnostic and not graded.
  4. For every wrong answer, read the adaptive feedback - it points you to a page in the OpenStax chapter or a timestamp in the video.
  5. Note which misconception category your errors fall into: constructivism means no guidance, behaviourism only applies to animals, or social constructivism ignores individual cognition.
  6. Revisit only the sections or timestamps you missed, then re-read the relevant reference card before class.
  7. Record your pre-test score - you will compare it with your post-test score in the synthesis phase.
  8. Complete the AI engagement task and post your prompt, the AI output and your evaluation to the LMS discussion board before the session.
  9. Flag any item whose feedback you disagree with; we debate those flagged items during the misconception review.
  10. Bring your AI prompt, its output and your written evaluation to class for the AI Critique Lab.

1 Round 1 — Theory Jigsaw

Students divide into five expert groups, one per theory (behaviourism, cognitivism, constructivism, social constructivism, humanism). Each expert group reads a short provided summary, uses an AI tool to generate an additional explanation, critically interrogates that output (what is accurate, what is oversimplified, what biases appear), and prepares a 3-minute peer-teaching summary on a shared Google Doc. Groups then reform into mixed-theory groups where each member teaches their theory. Roles rotate: facilitator, note-taker, AI critic and presenter. Timing: 12 minutes expert-group preparation, 15 minutes mixed-group teaching, 3 minutes debrief. This is a social-constructivist jigsaw - knowledge is distributed, negotiated and co-constructed.

🎓 Instructor guidance on critical AI use
  • Require students to draft their own comparison on paper before opening any AI tool, so the AI supplements independent reasoning rather than replacing it.
  • Make the boundary explicit: AI is appropriate for brainstorming, generating examples and drafting tables, but human judgement is essential for ethical decisions, cultural sensitivity and pedagogical fit.
  • Avoid unreflective reliance by requiring an AI interaction log and critical annotation for every prompt a group uses.
  • Model the four critical questions aloud when presenting the AI-generated lesson plan: which theory does this assume, what is missing, whose learning needs are excluded, and where does the AI make unsupported claims?
  • When a group accepts an AI suggestion, ask what they verified and against which source before it earns a place in their design.
  • Keep group roles rotating so no single student becomes the informal AI operator and everyone practises evaluation.

Section 7

Differentiation & Inclusivity

Multiple entry points, structured group roles and AI scaffolding ensure every learner can participate, while extension tasks stretch advanced learners and accessibility options remove barriers.

♿ Support
  • Multiple entry points through visual, verbal and kinesthetic group roles so every learner has a route into the task.
  • Peer mentoring within mixed-theory groups, with stronger students supporting explanation and critique.
  • Structured roles for collaborative work: facilitator, note-taker, AI critic and presenter, rotated each activity.
  • AI scaffolding for struggling learners through simplified explanations and step-by-step guidance, always checked against the readings.
  • Accessibility accommodations: captioned videos with transcripts, screen-reader compatible documents, and options to contribute in written, oral or multimodal form.
  • Flexible grouping and clear AI-use guidance to reduce anxiety for students with less AI experience, with optional tutorials available.
🚀 Extension
  • Lead an AI bias analysis of a widely used educational AI tool and present the findings to the class.
  • Design a complex interdisciplinary learning activity that integrates three or more theories with a documented AI critique.
  • Mentor peers in critiquing AI-generated content, modelling the four critical questions.
  • Compare how the same theory is framed across cultures and languages by prompting AI in more than one language and auditing the differences.
