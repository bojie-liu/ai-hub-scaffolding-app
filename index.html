Introduction to Learning Theories

A Comprehensive Lesson Plan with AI Integration

Duration: 150 minutes | Level: Teacher Education

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  1. Explain the key principles of behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism.
  2. Compare and contrast the three learning theories using appropriate terminology.
  3. Apply learning theories to real-world educational scenarios.
  4. Critically evaluate AI-generated educational content for accuracy, bias, and completeness.
  5. Articulate independent reasoning about when and how to use AI in learning.
Pedagogical Approaches:
  • Social Constructivism – Collaborative knowledge building through peer dialogue
  • Scaffolded Instruction – Gradual release from teacher guidance to student independence
  • Peer Assessment – Students evaluate each other's work using rubrics
  • Project-Based Learning (PBL) – Applied portfolio creation
  • Multiple Intelligences – Varied assessment formats (visual, verbal, intrapersonal)
🤖 AI Integration Focus

Students will critically engage with AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude) to:

  • Generate initial explanations and compare with authoritative sources
  • Identify AI biases, oversimplifications, and omissions
  • Modify AI outputs based on their own reasoning
  • Document decision-making when overriding AI suggestions

Pre-Class Preparation (Flipped Learning Component)

Pre-reading/Video Materials

  • Watch: Khan Academy – "Introduction to Learning Theories" (5 min)
  • Read: "Behaviorism, Cognitivism, Constructivism: A Comparison" (Chapter 1 from Learning Theories for Teachers, Ormrod)
  • Collaborative Annotation: Use Hypothesis.is to annotate the reading with questions, comments, and connections to your experiences (Social Constructivism approach)

Pre-Test / Diagnostic Quiz

📋 Quiz: Learning Theories Pre-Test (10 questions)

Purpose: Assess prior knowledge and identify misconceptions

Format: Multiple choice with adaptive feedback

  • Correct answers: Receive links to extension resources
  • Incorrect answers: Trigger a short video explainer with guided re-quiz
  • Misconception analysis: "Which learning theory is most associated with rewards and punishments?" (common conflation of behaviorism and cognitivism)

Pedagogy-Specific Guiding Questions

For Constructivism

"Identify one example from your own life where you learned something not because you were taught, but because you constructed meaning from experience. Which theory best explains this?"

For Peer Assessment Preparation

"After watching the video, list two strengths and two weaknesses you see in each theory. Be ready to defend your evaluation in class."

🤖 Pre-Class AI Engagement Task

Instructions:

  1. Open ChatGPT (or your preferred AI tool) and prompt it: "Explain the difference between behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism in simple terms."
  2. Copy the response into a shared Google Doc.
  3. Reflect and record:
    • "Did the AI accurately represent all three theories? Did it miss any key concepts?"
    • "Did the AI show any bias (e.g., favoring one theory over another)? If so, how?"
    • "What does this tell you about the limitations of AI-generated educational content?"
  4. Bring your observations to class.

Teaching & Learning Activities (150 minutes)

Introduction (15 minutes – 10% of class time)

Pedagogy-Aligned Hook (Social Constructivism)

Provocative scenario: "A student fails math every year but excels in basketball. According to learning theory, why?"

  • Students pair-share their initial explanations
  • Instructor collects responses on Padlet (constructivist collaborative board)

Pre-Test Review with Misconception Analysis

Display top 3 misconceptions from the pre-test (e.g., "Cognitivism is the same as behaviorism because both focus on internal mental states")

  1. Go to www.mentimeter.com
  2. Create a new presentation and add a "Multiple Choice" question: "Which of these is actually a misconception?"
  3. Display the join code on the projector
  4. Students visit menti.com and enter the code on their devices
  5. Live poll results appear; instructor reveals correct answer and explains reasoning

Real-World Connection

"Understanding these theories will help you design more effective lessons and adapt to different learners. Today, we will also use AI to explore how these theories apply—and where AI might misrepresent them."

🤖 AI-Generated Scenario Introduction

Show an AI-generated explanation of a learning theory from ChatGPT (pre-created by instructor):

"Behaviorism says learning is just stimulus-response, so teachers just need to reward good behavior."

Ask students: "Is this accurate? What is missing?"

  • Discuss the oversimplification: behaviorism also includes extinction, generalization, and cognitive elements in modern versions
  • Model critical questioning: Instructor demonstrates how to challenge AI: "Does behaviorism account for thinking, or only behavior? Cite a source."
Development Activities (105 minutes – 70% of class time)

Chunk 1: Behaviorism & Cognitivism (35 minutes)

Interactive Lecture with Think-Pair-Share

  • Instructor explains key concepts (classical vs. operant conditioning, information processing model) using animated slides
  • After 10 minutes, pause: "Think of two examples of cognitivism in a classroom. Pair-share with a neighbor. Then we discuss."
🤖 AI-Enabled Activity (Structured AI Interaction)

Task: In pairs, students prompt ChatGPT/Claude: "Give me a real-life example of classical conditioning in education."

Critique: Evaluate the AI's example. Does it match the definition? Is it accurate? (Many AI examples conflate classical and operant conditioning.)

Modify: Pairs rewrite the AI's example to correct any errors, documenting changes.

Share: One pair presents the original AI output and their corrected version, explaining reasoning.

📝 Quiz: Conditioning Scenarios – Peer Assessment

Students answer 3 multiple-choice scenarios, then swap answers with a partner and assess using a simple rubric (accuracy, justification).

Chunk 2: Constructivism & Social Learning (35 minutes)

Jigsaw Activity (Social Constructivism)

  • Groups of 4. Each member becomes "expert" on one aspect:
    • Piaget's cognitive constructivism
    • Vygotsky's social constructivism
    • Bandura's social learning theory
    • Situated learning
  • Experts meet and prepare a 2-minute summary. Then return to home group and teach each other.
🤖 AI-Enabled Activity (Collaborative AI-Assisted Problem-Solving)

Scenario: "A student is struggling to understand fractions in math class. Which constructivist approach would you recommend, and why?"

AI support: Each group prompts an AI tool: "Give me three constructivist teaching strategies for fractions."

Critique: Groups evaluate each AI suggestion for:

  • Alignment with constructivist principles (e.g., does it promote active meaning-making?)
  • Practicality in a real classroom
  • Potential biases (does AI favor one culture's approach?)

Final product: Groups choose one AI suggestion and modify it to better fit their chosen constructivist theory, presenting rationale.

Chunk 3: Comparison & Synthesis (35 minutes)

Concept Mapping (Scaffolded Instruction)

  • Instructor provides a partially completed concept map (graphic organizer) comparing the three theories
  • Students work in groups to fill in missing elements (key theorists, assumptions, role of teacher, role of assessment)
  • Gradual release: First model one example, then groups complete the rest, then peer-check with adjacent group
🤖 AI-Enabled Synthesis Task

Task: Each group prompts AI: "Create a comparison table of behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism including: key theorists, unit of analysis, role of experience, and typical critiques."

Critical interrogation:

  • Does the table include all required columns?
  • Are the critiques accurate? (Many AI tables omit the criticism that behaviorism ignores internal states.)
  • Modify: Groups add missing critiques or correct inaccuracies, documenting why they changed AI's output
  • Share: Gallery walk – groups post their tables on walls; others use sticky notes to add comments ("I agree with your change because…")
Synthesis & Closure (15 minutes – 10% of class time)

Post-Test (Misconception Check)

Same 3 misconception items from the pre-test. Students answer again via Mentimeter. Instructor shows comparison of pre- and post-class results to highlight growth.

Peer-Teaching Summaries

  • Each student writes a 1-minute summary of the biggest thing they learned today
  • They share with a neighbor, who adds one insight
  • Three volunteers share with the class
🤖 AI Reflection Discussion

Class discussion prompts:

  • "How did AI support or hinder your learning today? Share one instance where AI was helpful and one where it was misleading."
  • "When did you need to override AI suggestions? What did that feel like?"

Instructor highlights key lessons: "AI can summarize but it oversimplifies. Your human judgment is essential to evaluate, correct, and contextualize."

Preview of Next Session

"Next time, we will apply these theories to designing a lesson plan. Bring a topic you love to teach."

Assessment Methods

Formative Assessment (During Class)

Pedagogy-Aligned Observations

  • Constructivism: Instructor circulates during jigsaw and concept mapping, noting quality of group dialogue, use of peer explanations, and evidence of meaning-making
  • Peer Assessment: Rubric checklist used by students during scenario quizzes (accuracy, justification, clarity)

Real-time Polling

🎯 Mentimeter Exit Ticket

Question: "Which theory still confuses you? What is one question you have?"

Setup:

  1. Create an "Open Ended" question in Mentimeter
  2. Students submit responses anonymously
  3. Word cloud displays common themes
  4. Instructor addresses top confusions in next class
🤖 AI-Supported Formative Checks

Students submit their AI interaction logs (AI outputs + their critiques) via Google Form.

Instructor reviews critical engagement:

  • Are students identifying biases?
  • Are they modifying outputs intelligently?

Quick feedback: Instructor selects 2–3 examples and shares anonymized exemplars of strong critical engagement during the closure discussion.

Summative Assessment

Pedagogy-Aligned Approach: Project-Based Learning (PBL) + Multiple Intelligences

Learning Theory Portfolio includes:

  1. A concept map comparing the three theories (visual-spatial)
  2. A written reflection on how each theory applies to their own learning (intrapersonal)
  3. A recorded 5-minute podcast explaining one theory to a peer (verbal-linguistic)
  4. A lesson plan snippet applying one theory to a specific topic (logical-mathematical)

Rubric (Pedagogy-Linked Criteria)

  • Constructivism: Depth of personal meaning-making in reflection (30%)
  • Peer Assessment: Evidence of evaluating others' work based on rubric (20%)
  • PBL: Completeness and coherence of portfolio as a whole (20%)
  • Critical AI Engagement (30%) – see below
🤖 AI-Integrated Assessment Requirements

Requirement: Students may use AI to brainstorm or generate initial drafts for any portfolio component, but must:

  1. Disclose which sections used AI, and for what purpose
  2. Evaluate AI outputs: For each AI-related component, submit a short paragraph explaining:
    • "What did the AI suggest? What was accurate? What was missing or biased?"
    • "What changes did you make? Why did you override the AI's suggestion?"
    • "Where did your own human judgment prove essential?"
  3. Document their decision-making process in a table format
Example Documentation Table:
AI-Generated Content Did You Use It? Modifications Made Rationale (Why human judgment took precedence)
Example comparison table Modified Added missing critique about behaviorism ignoring internal states AI omitted this; I know it's a standard critique from our reading
Podcast script draft Rejected entirely I wrote my own script because AI's tone was too formal for a peer audience AI didn't understand the context
Rubric – "Critical AI Engagement" (30% of total)
  • 90–100%: Excellent evaluation of AI outputs; clear evidence of independent judgment; identifies subtle biases; demonstrates how human reasoning improved the final product
  • 70–89%: Good evaluation; some modifications but limited depth; most AI suggestions accepted without strong rationale
  • 50–69%: Minimal evaluation; AI used uncritically; no documentation of modifications
  • <50%: No disclosure; AI use hidden; no evidence of critical engagement

Differentiation & Inclusivity

Pedagogy-Tailored Supports

  • Multiple Intelligences: Portfolio options cater to visual, verbal, intrapersonal, logical learners
  • Constructivism: Struggling learners receive additional sentence starters for AI critique ("The AI said…, but I think… because…")
  • Peer Assessment: Differentiated peer-rubric – simpler version for novice assessors (3 criteria) vs. advanced version (5 criteria)

Extension Activities for Advanced Learners

  • Create a mini-lesson plan using TPACK framework integrating AI tools (beyond basic portfolio)
  • Evaluate AI-generated lesson plans for a specific theory and propose improvements
  • Prompt AI to generate counterarguments to the theories (e.g., "Criticize constructivism from a behaviorist perspective"), then evaluate the critique
🤖 AI-Supported Differentiation

Struggling Learners

  • AI tools provide simplified explanations (e.g., "Explain behaviorism like I'm 10") with guided prompts to evaluate accuracy
  • Instructor pre-loads a "safe" AI prompt template
  • Step-by-step AI prompt guide with examples

Advanced Learners

  • Prompt AI to generate counterarguments to the theories
  • Open-ended AI challenges with minimal scaffolding
  • Create "AI vs. Human Debate": argue for human-centric vs. AI-assisted learning

Accessibility

  • AI-generated captions for all video materials (via auto-transcribe)
  • Text-to-speech for AI outputs (for students with reading difficulties)
  • Language translation via AI (e.g., Google Translate for non-native English speakers)

AI Familiarity Accommodations

  • Beginners: Step-by-step AI prompt guide with examples
  • Experienced: Open-ended AI challenges with minimal scaffolding

Universal Supports

  • Captioned videos for all pre-class materials
  • Tactile concept map templates for kinesthetic learners
  • Multiple formats for assessment (visual, audio, written, practical)

Reflection & Improvement

Pedagogy-Specific Success Indicators

  • Constructivism: Did students reference each other's ideas in discussions? Did AI critique move beyond "it's wrong" to "it's incomplete"?
  • Peer Assessment: Did students use rubric consistently? Were calibration exercises effective?
  • PBL: Portfolio completeness and depth of personal reflection

Student Feedback (Pedagogy-Aligned)

📊 TPACK Self-Evaluation Form

Students rate their own use of AI, critical thinking, and collaboration.

Questions:

  • "Which activity helped you understand the theories best?" (open-ended)
  • "Rate the AI tasks: Were they confusing, helpful, or just average?"
  • "Did you feel you could challenge the AI's outputs, or did you feel you had to accept them?" (Likert scale)
🤖 AI-Specific Reflection

Student Feedback on AI Integration

  • "Did AI tools enhance or distract from your learning? Provide one example of each."
  • "Were you able to maintain your own thinking when using AI? Explain."

Instructor Reflection on AI Effectiveness

  • Data: Number of students who identified AI bias in pre-class task
  • Critical incidents: Moments where students strongly disagreed with AI and defended their reasoning
  • Challenges: Students who accepted AI uncritically – what support do they need next time?

Data on Student Critical Engagement with AI

  • Collected from AI logs: instances where AI outputs were rejected, modified, or accepted with justification
  • Compare pre-class vs. in-class: Did students become more critical over time?

Modification Strategies Based on Pedagogical Theory

  • Constructivism: If students struggled to critique AI, add a structured "AI evaluation sentence starter" handout before the next class
  • Peer Assessment: If rubric scores were inconsistent, conduct a calibration session with AI-generated sample submissions
  • AI Integration: If too many students overly relied on AI, increase emphasis on independent judgment in future prompts (e.g., require students to answer first, then compare with AI)
  • Differentiation: If advanced learners felt under-challenged, add "AI vs. Human Debate" extension: students argue for human-centric vs. AI-assisted learning
