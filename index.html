EDU 3102 · Teacher Professionalism

Teacher Professionalism in Local and International Contexts

This lesson examines how teacher professionalism is defined, enacted and contested in local and international contexts. Students compare sociological models, national standards and global frameworks, then test an AI assistant's claims about professionalism against authoritative sources. AI runs through the lesson as a fallible co-analyst whose fluent answers must be verified, modified or rejected.

Section 1

Intended Learning Outcomes

Each outcome pairs a disciplinary goal with an explicit AI integration, so critical AI use is engineered into the learning rather than added on at the end.

ILODescription🤖 AI Integration
ILO1Explain the main sociological and policy models of teacher professionalism, including occupational and organisational professionalism, standards frameworks, reflective practice, collaborative professionalism, teacher agency and glocalisation.AI summarises each model on demand, but students must verify every summary against the cited source and flag distortions, omissions and invented detail.
ILO2Compare how professionalism is defined and enacted in at least one local context and two international contexts, naming shared and context-bound competences.AI drafts a comparison table; students check it for context errors and add the local competences the model leaves out.
ILO3Critically evaluate AI-generated claims about teacher professionalism, identifying inaccuracies, oversimplifications, cultural bias and confident-but-unsupported assertions.The AI Critique Lab supplies confident but partly flawed outputs that students must accept, modify or reject with a written justification.
ILO4Synthesise a personal, defensible stance on what teacher professionalism should mean and justify it with evidence in a reflective portfolio.AI supports drafting and structuring, but the argument, the judgement and the ethical reasoning remain the student's own.
🤖 Design principle

AI is treated as a fallible co-analyst. It is useful for drafting, summarising and comparison, but it is never an authority on teacher professionalism: every AI claim must be checked against a named source and marked as accepted, modified or rejected before it can be used.

Pre-Class Preparation

Before the session, students take a short diagnostic pre-test, complete two comparative readings, and critique one AI-generated summary of teacher professionalism.

📚 Required preparation
  • Read Evetts' account of occupational and organisational professionalism (linked in the footer) and note one example of each from your own schooling.
  • Skim one national teaching-standards document and one international framework (both linked in the footer) and list two competences they share and two they do not.
  • Read the short introduction to reflective practice (linked in the footer) and bring one classroom incident you would be willing to reflect on in a group.
  • Complete the diagnostic pre-test in your LMS (steps in the tool runbook below). It is ungraded and exists only to surface misconceptions before the session.
✅ Pre-class checklist
Tool Lesson tool runbook - set up the LMS diagnostic pre-test, Mentimeter, Padlet, Kahoot and Google Forms
  1. LMS pre-test - in your course in Blackboard, Moodle or Canvas, create a quiz named 'Teacher Professionalism - diagnostic pre-test'.
  2. LMS pre-test - set it to 4 multiple-choice items, one attempt, untimed and ungraded at 0 per cent, so it is purely diagnostic.
  3. LMS pre-test - enter the four items from the pre-test bank and tag each one with its misconception category: core concept, comparative context or application.
  4. LMS pre-test - switch on 'show feedback after submission' and set a due date 24 hours before the workshop.
  5. LMS pre-test - after the deadline, export the item statistics, note which categories the cohort missed, and reuse those same categories in the post-test.
  6. Mentimeter - create a word-cloud question, 'In one word, what does teacher professionalism mean where you teach?', plus a multiple-choice item asking whether professionalism is local or universal. See the Mentimeter link in the footer.
  7. Mentimeter - open the poll in the first five minutes of the workshop, project the live results, and screenshot the cloud as the baseline for the framing discussion.
  8. Padlet - create a board titled 'Local and international professionalism cases' with four columns: Local case, International case, Shared competence, Context-bound competence. See the Padlet link in the footer.
  9. Padlet - share the board link with students; each group posts its case evidence and tags every claim with the source it came from.
  10. Padlet - return to the board in the closing debrief to surface exactly where local and international definitions diverge.
  11. Kahoot - build a 10-question review quiz from the six professionalism models and the scenario bank, and run it as a low-stakes retrieval warm-up before the post-test. See the Kahoot link in the footer.
  12. Kahoot - switch to assignment mode if you want it completed asynchronously, and read the report to spot the model the cohort confused most.
  13. Google Forms - create a three-item exit ticket: which model changed your thinking, which AI claim did you reject and why, and what will you do differently. See the Google Forms link in the footer.
  14. Google Forms - enable response summaries and read them alongside the LMS post-test to plan the follow-up seminar.

Teaching & Learning Activities

A 105-minute run sheet: a comparative case jigsaw, an AI Critique Lab, a synthesis chat, and the post-test - each with an explicit AI touchpoint.

1 Round 1 — Comparative case analysis (jigsaw)

In groups of four, each member takes one case - two are local, two are international. Members become the expert on their own case, then teach it back to the group, which compares all four. Groups work through the peer-work checklist, post their evidence to the Padlet case wall, and record observations for the instructor journal. Timing: 5 min brief, 12 min expert reading, 15 min teach-back, 3 min wrap.

🎓 Instructor guidance on critical AI use
  • Model the critique live: read one AI claim aloud and ask the class what would count as evidence for it before anyone judges it.
  • Insist on a named source for every claim. Treat a confident tone as a warning sign rather than proof, and say so out loud.
  • Use the AI decision log in the portfolio to make students' accept, modify and reject reasoning visible and therefore assessable.
  • Watch for automation bias: students who accept fluent AI summaries without checking. Intervene with a counter-example drawn from a different national context.
  • Debrief the comparison explicitly: ask which competences the frameworks treat as universal and which are bound to a particular context, and why that distinction matters for their own practice.

Section 7

Differentiation & Inclusivity

Multiple entry points, scaffolds for students who need them, and extensions for those ready to go further - all mapped to the same outcomes.

♿ Support
  • Provide all readings in advance in accessible formats, including a screen-reader-friendly PDF and adjustable text size, plus a glossary of comparative-education terms.
  • Offer a scaffolded critique template with four fixed columns: claim, source, verdict of accept, modify or reject, and justification.
  • Assign group roles - chair, source-checker, AI-sceptic and note-taker - so no student carries the whole cognitive load.
  • Allow reflection to be drafted in a first language with a short English summary, for students working across languages.
  • Work through one complete critique block together before students attempt the lab independently.
🚀 Extension
  • Extend the comparison to a third national system and present a five-minute policy brief to the cohort.
  • Trace one competence from an international framework through to a local policy document and map exactly what changed in translation.
  • Design an appraisal or professional-development model that balances organisational accountability with occupational trust, and defend it against the limitations in the reference cards.
  • Lead a peer lesson-study cycle in your placement school and report the evidence of impact to the group.
