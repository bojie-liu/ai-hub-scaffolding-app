Intended Learning Outcomes (ILOs)

By the end of this 150-minute session, students will be able to:

1

Analyze and compare the core principles of Behaviorism, Cognitivism, and Constructivism using collaborative discussion and peer critique.
Social Constructivism

2

Apply the key tenets of each theory to evaluate a real-world educational scenario in a small group, justifying their theoretical selection with evidence from the literature.

3

Co-construct a shared conceptual map of learning theories within their learning community, integrating multiple perspectives.
Social Constructivism

4

Critically evaluate AI-generated explanations of learning theories, identifying strengths, limitations, and potential biases in the AI's output.
AI Literacy

5

Demonstrate independent judgment by deciding when, whether, and how to use or modify AI-generated content to deepen their understanding of learning theories.
AI Literacy

AI Integration across ILOs 4 & 5: These outcomes are deliberately framed as critical evaluation and independent judgment — never uncritical use. Every AI touchpoint in this lesson is paired with a human-interrogation task (accept / modify / reject, with justification).

Pre-Class Preparation

Flipped-learning component — completed on the LMS before the 150-minute session.

📖 Pre-Reading ~30 min

Read the chapter "Theoretical Perspectives on Learning" (posted on the LMS).

🧠 Diagnostic Quiz 5 questions

A short MCQ quiz checking existing knowledge of key terms — Classical Conditioning, Schema, Zone of Proximal Development, and two further terms.

Adaptive feedback: Based on incorrect answers, students are routed to the specific pre-reading section that addresses the missed concept.

💬 Pedagogy-Specific Guiding Question Social Constructivism

"As you read, find one example from your own life (or a hypothetical classroom) that you think best illustrates each of the three theories. Be ready to share this with your small group."

🤖 AI Pre-Class Task AI Literacy

Interact with an AI tool (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, or Bing Chat) and ask it to compare the three theories in simple terms.

Explain the difference between behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism in simple terms.

Guided Reflection Prompt

Write 2–3 sentences on what the AI got right, what it might have missed, and whether its explanation seemed biased toward any one theory. Did it oversimplify a concept?

Why this matters: This pre-task creates a shared, lived experience of AI fallibility that the in-class critique activities (Sections 3 & 4) build upon — students arrive already having noticed limitations.

🚀 Introduction 20 min

Pedagogy-Aligned Hook 5 min

Provocative Problem: "A student is failing math. Should we (a) give them more rewards for completing homework, (b) teach them better note-taking and memory strategies, or (c) let them work on real-world problems with peers to discover math concepts themselves?"

Raise hands for each option. This primes the need for theory — each option maps to a different theory of learning.

Pre-Test Review 5 min

Quick poll on three key concepts to surface common misconceptions from the diagnostic quiz. The instructor uses a think-pair-share format to address the top 2–3 misconceptions collaboratively.

Real-World Connection 5 min

"Your future students will learn in complex ways. Understanding these theories is like having a tool belt — you'll need to choose the right tool for the task."

AI Integration: AI Scenario Introduction 5 min AI Literacy

  • Activity: Display a 3-paragraph AI-generated summary of "How Students Learn" on screen.
  • Task: "Read this summary generated by an AI. With the person next to you, identify one statement that is accurate, one that is misleading, and one potential bias. For example, does it over-emphasize technology or ignore social learning?"
  • Purpose: Models critical questioning of AI outputs from the very start.
🔧 Run the pre-test poll with Mentimeter

Purpose: Surface misconceptions in real time and give every one of the 90 students a voice in a low-stakes format.

Learning objective: ILO 1 (analyze/compare) — activate prior knowledge before the jigsaw.

  1. Go to Mentimeter and log in (or create a free account).
  2. Click "New presentation" → "Add slide" → choose the Multiple Choice or Word Cloud slide type.
  3. Enter the three concept questions drawn from the diagnostic quiz (e.g., "Which term best describes 'learning as a change in observable behavior'?").
  4. Go to Present and display the join code on the main screen (menti.com + code).
  5. Ask students to open menti.com on their phones and enter the code.
  6. Give 60 seconds; watch responses appear live, then reveal the chart to launch think-pair-share on the most-missed item.

🧩 Activity 1 — Jigsaw Expert Groups 40 min Social Constructivism

Students are divided into three "Expert Groups" (30 students each):

Expert Group A

Behaviorism

Expert Group B

Cognitivism

Expert Group C

Constructivism

Task: Expert Fact Sheet

Each group collaborates to create a one-page "Expert Fact Sheet" on their theory. It must include:

  • 3 core principles
  • 2 key theorists
  • 1 classroom application
  • 1 limitation of the theory

AI-Integrated Activity 15 min of this block AI Literacy

As a group, use one AI tool (e.g., ChatGPT) to generate a list of potential classroom activities that align with your theory. For example: "Give me 5 classroom activities based on Behaviorism."

Critical Interrogation Task — do NOT simply copy the AI's suggestions. Your group must:

  1. Criticize each AI suggestion. Is it truly aligned with your theory? Is it too simplistic or outdated? Does it contain a potential bias (e.g., favoring technology)?
  2. Refine at least 2 of the AI's activities. Modify them to be more pedagogically sound or more appropriate for adult learners.
  3. Document your decision: for each AI suggestion, note whether you accepted it as-is, modified it, or rejected it — and why.
Instructor guidance (circulate and prompt): "Why did you override that AI suggestion? What human judgment did you use that the AI lacked?" "Is the AI just repeating a textbook definition, or is it adding new insight?"
🔧 Capture group decisions with Padlet

Purpose: Give each of the three expert groups a visible, shared wall where they post accepted / modified / rejected AI suggestions — making the critique process public and comparable.

Learning objective: ILO 2 & ILO 5 — apply theory and document independent judgment.

  1. Go to Padlet and sign in.
  2. Click "Make a Padlet" → choose the Wall or Columns format.
  3. Create three columns titled Accepted, Modified, Rejected.
  4. Under Share, set privacy to "Secret" or "Anyone with the link can write", then copy the link.
  5. Share the link in the LMS / chat. Each expert group posts a card per AI suggestion with a one-line justification.
  6. Project all three walls side-by-side for a rapid cross-group comparison at minute 40.
🔧 Run a knowledge check with Kahoot!

Purpose: A fast, gamified mid-point check that each expert group has correctly internalized the core principles of its theory.

Learning objective: ILO 1 — analyze and compare core principles.

  1. Go to Kahoot! and log in, then click "Create".
  2. Add 5 questions — one per theory plus two comparison items.
  3. Set a 20-second timer per question; save and click "Play" → "Teach".
  4. Display the game PIN. Students join at kahoot.it.
  5. Run the quiz in team mode (one device per expert group) to reinforce collaboration.

🏠 Activity 2 — Jigsaw Home Groups & Concept Mapping 45 min Social Constructivism

Students reorganize into "Home Groups" (30 groups of 3 students — one from each expert group).

Task: Teach Your Theory

Each member teaches their theory to the group.

AI-Enhanced Peer Teaching: "Briefly show your group the AI-generated activity suggestions you modified. Explain why you changed them. This helps others see how AI can be a starting point, not the final answer."

Collaborative Concept Map

Groups co-construct a visual concept map comparing the three theories (e.g., using Miro or a large sheet of paper). They label key similarities and differences.

AI Integration & Bridging 20 min of this block AI Literacy

Create a concept map comparing behaviorism, cognitivism, and constructivism.

Critical Comparison Task: Compare the AI's map to your own.

  • Which elements did the AI prioritize?
  • Which connections did it miss?
  • What does this tell you about the human role in synthesizing complex knowledge?

Groups annotate their own map with notes on what the AI did better or worse.

Scaffolding & cross-pedagogy elements: Throughout, the instructor uses short interactive lectures (with think-pair-share) to clarify tricky concepts. Animated visualizations (e.g., Pavlov's Dog, Atkinson–Shiffrin Memory Model, Vygotsky's ZPD) are shown. A digital flashcard deck is available via the LMS for independent review.
🔧 Co-construct the concept map in Miro

Purpose: Provide a shared digital canvas where home groups build and compare maps simultaneously.

Learning objective: ILO 3 — co-construct a shared conceptual map.

  1. Go to Miro and log in.
  2. Click "+ New board" and choose the Mind Map or blank template.
  3. Create one frame per home group (Board → Frames → drop 30 frames, or duplicate a group template).
  4. Set board sharing to "Anyone with the link can edit" and distribute via LMS.
  5. Ask each group to place the three theories as central nodes and add sticky-note connections for similarities/differences.
  6. After the AI comparison, ask groups to add red sticky notes labelled "AI missed this" and green ones labelled "AI got this right".

🎯 Synthesis & Closure 25 min

Post-Test 5 min Formative

A short, scenario-based quiz that targets the initial misconceptions from the pre-test.

Peer-Teaching Reflection 10 min

In their home groups, students share one "aha!" moment from today. One student from each group shares a key takeaway with the whole class.

AI Reflection Discussion 10 min AI Literacy

Class-Wide Discussion: "Today we used AI in several ways. Let's reflect:"

  • "Was there a time when the AI's suggestions were genuinely unhelpful or misleading? How did you handle that?"
  • "When did you feel it was essential to use your own human judgment to override the AI? Was it about accuracy, ethics, or context?"
  • "How can AI support social constructivism (collaborative learning) rather than replace it?"
Instructor models: "For example, when a group in Expert Group A rejected the AI's suggestion of a 'sticker chart' as the only behaviorist activity, they added a 'self-monitoring' component. That was a critical human judgment about developmental psychology that the AI missed."

Preview

"Next week, we will apply these theories to design a single lesson plan. Bring your concept maps and your AI notes."

Assessment Methods

Formative assessment during class · Summative pedagogy-aligned portfolio entry.

🔄 Formative Assessment During Class

Pedagogy-Aligned Observations

The instructor observes group dynamics and the depth of discussion in Jigsaw groups. Are students truly co-creating their fact sheets?

Real-Time Polling

Used during the Introduction and Synthesis for quick checks on concept clarity (see Mentimeter instructions above).

Peer Assessment

During the concept map activity, home groups use a simple checklist to assess each expert member's teaching clarity.

AI-Supported Formative Check AI Literacy

  • Task: Students submit a 2-sentence reflection via the LMS: "What AI suggestion did your group modify and why?"
  • Instructor review: The instructor reviews these submissions for evidence of critical engagement. Are students merely accepting AI output? Are they identifying bias or accuracy issues? This gives a quick snapshot of AI-literacy development.
🔧 Collect the formative reflection via Google Forms

Purpose: A rapid, sortable snapshot of whether students are engaging critically or passively with AI output.

Learning objective: ILO 4 — critically evaluate AI-generated explanations.

  1. Open Google Forms → Blank form.
  2. Title: "AI Suggestion — Accept, Modify, or Reject?"
  3. Add a Short answer: "Which AI suggestion did your group modify, and why?"
  4. Add a Multiple choice: "Did your group accept, modify, or reject it?" (Accepted / Modified / Rejected).
  5. In Responses, open the linked Google Sheet and sort by response type to see the distribution at a glance.
  6. Use the responses to select 2–3 exemplary critiques to read aloud in the AI reflection discussion.

🎓 Summative Assessment — "The Real-World Application Report" Pedagogy-Aligned Portfolio Entry

Task

  1. Choose a real or hypothetical learning scenario (e.g., teaching a language, learning a musical instrument, training in a corporate setting).
  2. Provide an analysis from the perspective of two of the three learning theories.
  3. AI-Integrated Requirement (Mandatory):
    Use an AI tool to generate a draft analysis of your scenario from one theoretical perspective.

    Critical Engagement Documentation — submit a document with two columns:

    • Column A: AI-Generated Text (from your prompts).
    • Column B: Your Critique & Modification.

    You must explicitly discuss:

    • What parts of the AI's analysis were accurate? Inaccurate?
    • What limitations or biases did the AI have?
    • Where did you override the AI and rely on your own human judgment? Why?
    • What part of your own analysis is superior to the AI's, and why?
  4. Conclude by justifying which theoretical framework you would prioritize and why.

Rubric Criteria

Factual Accuracy — 30%

Correct application of theoretical principles.

Critical Analysis — 30%

Depth of comparison between theories; identification of trade-offs.

Critical AI Engagement — 30%

Quality of critique; evidence of independent judgment; documentation of decision-making.

Written Communication — 10%

Clarity and structure.

🤝 AI-Supported Feedback & Learning Support Summative Context AI Literacy

Process

Students submit their report drafts to an AI-powered feedback tool (e.g., Gradescope's Feedback Studio with an AI rubric) designed by the instructor.

AI Role

The AI provides initial, structured feedback on factual accuracy — e.g., "Your application of 'operant conditioning' is correct, but you missed the role of the 'antecedent.'"

Instructor Oversight & Student Interrogation

  1. Instructor review: The instructor reviews and adjusts all AI-generated feedback before releasing it to students, to ensure it is pedagogically sound and not misleading.
  2. Student task: "After reading the AI's feedback on your draft, write a 1-paragraph reflection answering: 'Do you agree with the AI's assessment? What might it have missed about your argument? Where did you need to use your own judgment to overrule its feedback?'"
  3. Calibration: In the following tutorial, the instructor facilitates a 10-minute session where students share one example of where the AI feedback was helpful and one where it was not. This builds a shared understanding of AI's limits.
Human-in-the-loop principle: No AI-generated feedback reaches a student without instructor review. The AI is a first-pass annotator; the instructor remains the pedagogical authority, and students remain the final judges of their own argument.

Differentiation & Inclusivity

Pedagogy-tailored supports, AI-supported differentiation, and accessibility accommodations.

🧩 Pedagogy-Tailored Supports Social Constructivism

Struggling Learners

AI tools can generate simplified explanations or step-by-step summaries of a theory on demand. The instructor directs these students to use this as a starting scaffold before group discussion.

Explain Vygotsky's ZPD in three sentences with an example.

Advanced Learners

The instructor provides a prompt to deepen critical thinking:

Argue against your assigned learning theory's core principles using the perspective of another theory. What are the weaknesses?

♿ AI-Supported Differentiation AI Literacy

Accessibility

  • AI-generated captions for any videos shown.
  • Text-to-speech for the pre-reading.
  • AI-powered language translation for non-native speakers (e.g., Google Translate integration).

Varying AI Familiarity

  • The instructor provides a "Prompt Bank" (e.g., "5 Prompts to Analyze a Theory") for students less comfortable with AI tools, while advanced users can craft their own.
  • Clear guidance: "If you've never used AI for learning, start with the suggested prompts."

🦾 Accessibility Accommodations

  • Captioned videos
  • Large-print handouts
  • Alternative group roles (e.g., timekeeper, note-taker) as needed

Reflection & Improvement

How we know it worked, and what we change if it didn't.

📈 Pedagogy-Specific Success Indicators

  • Social Constructivism: Did groups engage in authentic, critical dialogue? Were concept maps co-constructed, not just copied from a single source? (Observational data from the instructor.)
  • Collaboration Efficacy: Did all members contribute equally in Jigsaw groups? (Assessed via peer feedback.)

🗣️ Student Feedback Aligned with Pedagogical Goals

AI-Specific Reflection Question

"Did the AI tools help you understand the theories better, or did they distract you from building your own understanding? Were you able to maintain your own critical thinking while using AI?"

General

"Which activity (the Jigsaw, the concept map, the AI critique) most helped you learn? What could be improved?"

🔍 Instructor Reflection & Modification

Data on AI Critical Engagement

The instructor reviews students' "Column A vs. Column B" submissions and their 2-sentence formative reflections to assess whether the critical engagement criteria were met.

Reflective question: "Where did students struggle most with criticality? Did they simply accept AI's first suggestion? Did they identify bias? Did they understand when human judgment was essential?"

Modification Strategy

If students struggled with AI critical thinking, the next lesson will include a focused 10-minute workshop on "AI prompt crafting for critique" (e.g., "Write a prompt that forces the AI to identify its own limitations"). The AI prompts for the Jigsaw activity will be refined to be more specific, e.g.:

Generate a behaviorist activity that does *not* rely on rewards.
