# Detailed Lesson Plan: The Modern Software Developer – AI-Powered Workflows

## 1. Intended Learning Outcomes (ILOs)
*Aligned with Bloom's Taxonomy and the three pedagogies (Flipped Learning, PBL, Critical Inquiry Seminar)*

| ILO # | Outcome | Bloom's Level | Pedagogy Link |
|-------|---------|---------------|---------------|
| ILO1 | **Analyze** the limitations of traditional software development workflows (manual coding, debugging) and compare them with AI-assisted approaches. | Analyze | Critical Inquiry Seminar (compare/contrast, identify flaws) |
| ILO2 | **Apply** an iterative AI-assisted workflow (Plan → Generate with AI → Modify → Repeat) to solve a small software problem in a team. | Apply | Project-Based Learning (PBL) (hands-on, collaborative mini-project) |
| ILO3 | **Evaluate** the impact of AI tools on developer productivity and software democratization, considering ethical, social, and quality implications. | Evaluate | Critical Inquiry Seminar (weigh evidence, form judgment) |
| ILO4 | **Create** a documented workflow that demonstrates the effective use of an AI tool for code generation, including modifications and testing. | Create | PBL (produce artifact) |
| ILO5 | **Critique** a team's AI-generated code using principles of software engineering (correctness, security, maintainability). | Evaluate | Critical Inquiry Seminar + Peer Assessment (structured critique) |

*Note: ILO4 and ILO5 may be interchangeable; adjust based on class focus.*

## 2. Pre-Class Preparation (Flipped Learning Component)
*Distributed 48 hours before class via LMS (e.g., Moodle)*

### Required Materials
- **Video (10 min)**: "The Evolution of Software Development: From Waterfall to AI-Assisted" – created by instructor (hosted on Vimeo)
- **Reading (6 pages)**: "AI Code Generation: Promises, Pitfalls, and the New Developer Role" – adapted from Martin Fowler's blog and GitHub Copilot documentation (PDF in LMS)

### Pre-Test / Diagnostic Quiz (10 questions)
*Format: multiple-choice + short answer, with immediate feedback for each answer*
**Sample questions**:
1. What is the primary difference between traditional coding and AI-assisted coding? (A) Speed only, (B) Shift from writing to reviewing, (C) No need for testing, (D) AI replaces all human roles → Feedback: B is correct; explain why.
2. Which of the following is a common risk of using AI-generated code? (A) Too fast, (B) Potential security vulnerabilities, (C) Cannot be modified, (D) Always correct → Feedback: B.
3. Short answer: List two steps you would take after receiving AI-generated code before integrating it into a project.

### Guiding Questions (Critical Inquiry + PBL)
- "What are the key differences between a developer in 2010 and a developer in 2025 in terms of daily tasks?"
- "If AI generates 80% of the code, what is left for the human developer to do? Where is the value?"
- "Think of one real-world application (e.g., a web app) you have used. How might its development have changed with AI tools?"

*Students submit answers to guiding questions via LMS forum – serves as warm-up for in-class discussion.*

## 3. Teaching & Learning Activities (90 minutes)

### Introduction (12 minutes)
**3.1 Hook & Real-World Connection (5 min)**
- Display a side-by-side comparison: a classic "FizzBuzz" program written manually (30 lines, 10 minutes) vs. generated by ChatGPT in 2 seconds.
- **Prompt**: "How many of you have used AI to write code? What was your experience?" (Use Mentimeter poll: "I trust AI code completely / I check everything / I don't use it yet")
- **Real-world link**: "Microsoft reports that Copilot users complete tasks 55% faster. But is faster always better? Today we'll explore both the power and the responsibility."

**3.2 Pre-Test Review & Misconception Analysis (5 min)**
- Show aggregated quiz results: highlight most missed question (likely #2 on security).
- **Critical Inquiry prompt**: "Why do you think many of you chose the wrong answer? What assumption led you there?"
- Address misconception: "AI code is not automatically secure. In fact, studies show it can introduce subtle bugs because the model doesn't 'understand' context."

**3.3 Agenda & ILOs Preview (2 min)**
- "By the end of this session, you will be able to (ILO1) analyze traditional vs. AI workflows, (ILO2) apply an iterative AI-assisted process, (ILO3) evaluate the impact, and (ILO4) produce a documented workflow. These mirror what modern software teams do daily."

### Development Activities (66 minutes) – *Project-Based Learning with Critical Inquiry*

**Phase 1: Team Formation & Challenge Briefing (5 min)**
- Divide class into 6 teams of 5. Assign roles:
  - **Project Lead** – manages timeline
  - **AI Prompt Engineer** – formulates prompts
  - **Code Reviewer** – checks output for errors
  - **Tester** – verifies functionality
  - **Documenter** – records workflow and reflections
- **Scenario**: "Your team is a startup that needs a simple REST API endpoint for a user registration system (accept username and email, return a welcome message). You must use an AI code generation tool (ChatGPT or Copilot) to generate the initial code, then modify it to meet best practices (error handling, input validation, documentation). Your final deliverable: a 2-minute presentation of your workflow and a critical reflection on the process."

**Phase 2: Critical Inquiry Roundtable (10 min)**
- Before coding, teams discuss:
  - "What potential risks does AI-generated code introduce?" (security, licensing, correctness)
  - "How does AI code generation change the role of the junior developer? Does it make them more or less valuable?"
  - "What is 'democratization' in this context? Does availability of AI tools truly level the playing field?"
- Each team records 3 key points on a shared Google Doc (one per team). This activates pre-class knowledge and sets a critical lens.

**Phase 3: Iterative Workflow Application (35 min)**
- **Step 1 – Plan (5 min)**: Teams outline the desired endpoint, inputs, outputs, error cases. They write a prompt draft.
- **Step 2 – Generate with AI (8 min)**: Each team uses ChatGPT or Copilot (provided via shared lab computers or own devices) to generate the code. **Prompt suggestion**: "Write a Python Flask endpoint that accepts a POST request with json {'username':..., 'email':...} and returns a welcome message."
- **Step 3 – Modify & Review (15 min)**: Teams run the code (if possible via online interpreter) or manually review. They must make at least two modifications: add input validation, add a try-except block. Document each change and reason.
- **Step 4 – Test & Repeat (7 min)**: Teams simulate test cases. If bugs found, they repeat the cycle (adjust prompt, regenerate, modify). Document iterations.

*During this phase, instructor circulates, observing team dynamics, answering technical questions, and prompting critical thought: "Why did you choose that prompt? What would happen if the email field were missing?"*

**Phase 4: Peer-Critique & Gallery Walk (10 min)**
- Each team posts their documented workflow (Google Doc) and final code to a shared space (Padlet or LMS forum).
- Teams rotate to another team's poster (digital) and provide structured feedback using a rubric (see Assessment). Focus on:
  - Completeness of workflow (plan-generate-modify-test)
  - Quality of modifications (did they fix real issues?)
  - Depth of critical reflection (did they consider risks?)
- Teams note one strength and one area for improvement.

**Phase 5: Cross-Team Discussion (6 min)**
- Quick share-out: Instructor asks: "What was the most surprising issue you encountered?" and "Which team's workflow do you think was most effective and why?"
- This synthesizes learning and reinforces the PBL and critical inquiry goals.

### Synthesis & Closure (12 minutes)

**Post-Test (5 min)**
- Same 10-question quiz, administered via LMS. Compare with pre-test to gauge growth.
- Immediate results displayed (class average improvement). Celebrate growth.

**Team Reflection & Peer Teaching (5 min)**
- Each team chooses one "takeaway" to share: a lesson learned about AI code, a modification strategy, or a critical insight.
- Instructor records these on a shared whiteboard (Jamboard) – creates a collective knowledge artifact.

**Preview of Next Session (2 min)**
- "Next week we will dive deeper into **prompt engineering** – how to craft prompts that produce secure, efficient code. You'll bring your documented workflows to refine."
- **Optional**: Assign teams to refine their mini-project at home using a different AI tool (GitHub Copilot vs. Claude) and prepare a comparison.

## 4. Assessment Methods

### Formative Assessment (During Class)

| Method | Focus | Pedagogy Link |
|--------|-------|---------------|
| Real-time polling (Mentimeter) | Pre/post quiz, hook poll | Flipped Learning (check understanding from pre-class) |
| Instructor observation | Team collaboration, use of AI tool, critical questioning | PBL (team dynamics, problem-solving) + Critical Inquiry (questioning depth) |
| Peer-critique rubric (see below) | Quality of workflow and reflection | Critical Inquiry + PBL (structured critique) |
| Exit ticket | "One thing I now understand about AI-assisted development that I did not before." | Formative check for all pedagogies |

**Peer-Critique Rubric (5-point scale for each criterion)**

| Criterion | 1 – Emerging | 3 – Developing | 5 – Mastery |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|-------------|
| **Workflow Documentation** | Missing steps; no iteration shown | Basic steps but limited detail | Clear plan-generate-modify-test cycle with reflections |
| **Modification Quality** | No changes made or only cosmetic | One functional improvement | Multiple meaningful fixes (validation, error handling) |
| **Critical Reflection** | No mention of risks or trade-offs | Mentions one risk generically | Deep analysis of security, ethics, or reliability; connects to democratization |
| **Collaboration** | Roles unclear; one person works | Some roles but uneven work | All roles actively contribute; team supports each other |

### Summative Assessment
**Project Deliverable**: Each team submits a **workflow portfolio** including:
- Final code (commented)
- 2-3 paragraph reflection answering:
  1. How did the AI-assisted workflow differ from traditional coding?
  2. What risks did you identify and how did you mitigate them?
  3. Do you believe AI tools democratize software engineering? Why or why not?
- Peer-critique notes from at least one other team

**Grading Rubric (aligned with ILOs)**

| ILO | Criterion | Weight |
|-----|-----------|--------|
| ILO1 (Analyze) | Contrast of traditional vs. AI workflow in reflection | 20% |
| ILO2 (Apply) | Demonstration of iterative workflow (documented) | 25% |
| ILO3 (Evaluate) | Depth of evaluation in reflection (risks, democratization) | 25% |
| ILO4 (Create) | Quality of final code (correctness, modifications) | 20% |
| Collaboration & Peer Feedback | From observation and peer-critique rubric | 10% |

*Note: Summative score is team-based, with peer evaluation of individual contributions (using CATME tool if available).*

## 7. Differentiation & Inclusivity

| Need | Strategy | Pedagogy Alignment |
|------|----------|-------------------|
| **Novice coders** | Provide a starter template (Flask skeleton) and a list of prompt examples. Rotate roles so they can be "Documenter" or "Tester" initially. | PBL (scaffolded entry points) |
| **Advanced coders** | Extend task: add authentication, database integration, or refactor AI code to be production-ready. Lead the discussion on ethical implications. | Critical Inquiry (deeper analysis) |
| **English language learners** | Caption pre-class videos; glossary of terms (prompt, endpoint, validation) provided. Use simple sentence structures in handouts. | Flipped Learning (accessible materials) |
| **Visual learners** | Provide diagram of iterative workflow (plan-generate-modify-test) as a graphic organizer. | Universal design |
| **Kinesthetic learners** | Use physical cards (steps of workflow) that teams arrange on table before coding. | Multiple modalities |
| **Students with disabilities** | Ensure all digital materials are screen-reader compatible; allow extended time for pre-class if needed; audio recordings of readings available. | Inclusive design |

## 8. Reflection & Improvement

### Success Indicators (Pedagogy-Specific)
- **Flipped Learning**: At least 85% of students complete pre-class materials (tracked via LMS). Post-test average improvement >20%.
- **PBL**: All teams produce a documented workflow with at least two meaningful modifications. Peer-critique shows constructive, specific feedback.
- **Critical Inquiry**: During Roundtable and Gallery Walk, students spontaneously raise ethical or quality concerns (e.g., security, bias, licensing). At least one team challenges the premise of democratization.

### Student Feedback
- **End-of-class exit ticket**: "What was the most valuable part of today's session for your learning?" and "What could be improved?"
- **Anonymous survey (next day via LMS)**: Rate collaboration, clarity of instructions, usefulness of AI tool. Include open-ended: "Did the pre-class materials prepare you well for the in-class challenge?"
- **Critical Inquiry Self-Evaluation**: Students rate their own and team's depth of questioning on a 1-5 scale.

### Modification Strategies (Based on Theory)
- **If students struggle with AI tool usage**: Provide a pre-recorded demo of prompt engineering (2 min) during Phase 1. Add a "prompt doctor" role.
- **If Critical Inquiry is shallow**: Insert a structured debate: "Resolved: AI-generated code should not be used in production without a human review." Assign teams to pro/con.
- **If time runs short**: Reduce Phase 4 (Gallery Walk) to 3-point feedback only. Move some iteration to homework (flipped extension).
- **For future iterations (PBL)**: Increase complexity by adding a second iteration where teams must refactor another team's AI-generated code. This builds deeper collaborative learning.
